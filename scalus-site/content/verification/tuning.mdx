# Tuning Your Proof

Not every statement is decided at once. A check can come back inconclusive, or run until its time
limit. Three things can be tuned: the **budget** of steps Lean's machine runs each program, the
**data** the statement ranges over, and the **time** a check may take.

## The Budget

Lean runs each program of a statement for at most a budget of steps of its CEK machine, and
Blaster reasons about that bounded run.

- **A proof at any budget holds without the budget.** A run that needs more steps counts against
  the proof: a claim must return `true` within the budget, and a premise that has not finished
  counts as true. So too small a budget cannot prove a false statement.
- **Too small a budget gives a spurious counterexample.** Scalus replays it on its own CEK
  machine, sees that the statement holds there, and reports `Inconclusive`. The reason says how
  many steps would do.

### Let Scalus Find It

`check(statement)`, without a budget, searches for one (`Budget.Auto`). It starts at 100 steps.
Where Lean's counterexample is spurious, Lean's machine counts what each program does on it, and
the search goes on at the least budget at which the statement holds there. For a statement that
the vesting validator rejects an early withdrawal, the search went 100, 262, 649, 694, 1315, 1573,
1584, 1608, and proved it there: eight checks in 45 seconds.

The budget found is kept with the result, in `<Suite>.proofs.json`. A statement that has not
changed is taken as it was kept. When the code changes, the next search starts at the kept
budget, so a statement still decided there takes one check.

### Give It Yourself

`check(statement, budget = 4000)` checks at a fixed budget (`Budget.LeanSteps(4000)`). Use it when
you know the cost of the statement, or to save the search on a run without kept results.

- **Stay close above the worst path.** Proof cost grows faster than the budget. For a `gcd`
  property whose worst sample needs 203 steps, budget 250 proved in 2 seconds, 350 took 52
  seconds, and 500 did not finish.
- **The path that counts is that of the runs the statement is about.** A statement that a
  validator rejects a transaction needs the budget of the rejection, not that of a whole accepted
  spend. The vesting validator rejects an early withdrawal after about 1600 steps: the statement
  is proved in 12 seconds at budget 1700, and not at all at 12000.

<Callout type="warning">
  Do not derive budgets from `ExUnits`. Plutus charges for each `Eval` transition, while Lean's
  machine counts `Eval` and `Return`: a Lean budget is about 1.85 times Scalus's step count.
</Callout>

### Within a Bound

`Budget.Within(n)` proves a statement of the inputs whose runs end within `n` steps. A run that the
budget cuts is read in the statement's favour, so it is no counterexample. The result is
`ProvenWithin`, with the bound: a weaker statement, not a theorem. Use it for a statement that no
budget proves, to learn that it holds as far as the runs go.

## Limit the Data

A validator that walks a collection of the script context, such as the signatories, the inputs or
the outputs, walks it to its end. For a statement over every context, the solver can always propose
a longer collection than the budget's runs walk. Its counterexample is then spurious at every
budget, and the check runs until its time limit.

`forAllDataLimited` bounds the length of the collections of a variable:

```scala
forAllDataLimited[ScriptContext](ctx => ctx.txInfo.signatories.length <= 2)(ctx =>
    (isRefund(ctx) && !signedByTheCommitter(ctx)) ==>: fails(validator, ctx.toData)
)
```

- **The limit is of the shape of the values:** `maxCollectionLength(x) <= n`, the length of a
  collection of `x` compared with a number (`x.txInfo.inputs.length <= 3`), `isEmpty`, `nonEmpty`,
  and `forall` over a list, or over a map's keys or values, of the limits of each element, joined
  with `&&`. Any other condition belongs in the statement's premise; the compiler says so.
- **Each test checks the limits first**, with a walk that ends, so a walk over a limited list runs
  to its end. The statement is proved as a theorem of the values within the limits. With
  `Budget.Auto`, the search starts at the steps the programs take on the largest values the limits
  allow.
- **It says nothing of larger values.** Name the statement so, and keep the statement of every
  context beside it.

The HTLC suite does all three for the rules that depend on the signatories. It states each rule of
every context, and tags that test `Unfinished`. It states the rule again of the contexts of at most
two signatures, under the same name with `_limited`, where Lean proves it:

```scala
inline def forAllContextsMax2Signatures(inline body: ScriptContext => Prop | Boolean): Prop =
    forAllDataLimited[ScriptContext](ctx => ctx.txInfo.signatories.length <= 2)(body)

private val refundRejectedUnlessTheCommitterSignsLimited = verifier.statement(
  "htlc.refund.rejected_unless_the_committer_signs_limited",
  forAllContextsMax2Signatures(ctx =>
      (isRefund(ctx) && !signedByTheCommitter(ctx)) ==>: fails(validator, ctx.toData)
  )
)
```

The rules that the validator decides by the time, before it reads the signatories, need no limit:
see [Your First Proof](/docs/verification/first-proof#why-no-limit-is-needed-here).

## The Time Limit

A check has a time limit by the clock: ten minutes (`UplcBlaster.defaultTimeout`). A check that
reaches it is given up, and is `Inconclusive`. The reason says where the check was:

- **still running a program symbolically:** the budget lets a program run through a long walk.
  A smaller budget, or limited data, may cure it;
- **with the solver:** the statement is hard for the solver as it is stated.

To give a check another limit, pass the tactic yourself:

```scala
check(VerificationRequest(statement, UplcBlaster(Budget.Auto, lean, 20.minutes)))
```

- With `Budget.Auto`, the limit is that of the whole search. `withAttemptTimeout(t)` adds one for
  each check of the search.
- `withoutTimeout` lifts the limit.
- `withMaxHeartbeats(n)` sets Lean's own limit of work for one command, twice Lean's default
  unless you say otherwise; `0` means none. It ends a check the same way on every machine, but the
  solver's time does not count towards it.

## Mark What Does Not Finish

Tag a test of a statement that Lean does not finish with `Unfinished`. Such a test waits for its
time limit, and the solver can take gigabytes meanwhile. `verify` leaves these tests out, and so
should `test` (see [sbt Commands](/docs/verification/sbt-commands#tests-that-lean-does-not-finish)).
They stay in the suite as the record of what is not proved yet, and `testOnly` runs them on purpose.

To see what Lean is given for a check that does not finish, keep the checks
(`SCALUS_LEAN_KEEP_CHECKS`) and run one by hand: see
[Looking at a Check](/docs/verification/lean-workspace#looking-at-a-check).
